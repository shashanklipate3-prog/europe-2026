Why not Oktoberfest?

It is genuinely running during your trip — closing weekend is 3–4 October. Catching it would mean gutting Amsterdam to a single night and swapping Berlin for Munich. Two reasons not to: at eight weeks out, tent table reservations for the final weekend are long gone and Munich beds for six on the closing Saturday are both scarce and priced accordingly. And for a group whose stated brief is "party", Berlin's club scene beats a beer tent you can't get a table in. Noted so you know it was considered, not missed.

What you're actually doing

Amsterdam · 3 nights

Rent your own electric boat and drive it yourselves down the canals with your own drinks on board — €17 each for two hours and the single best group photo of the trip. Van Gogh and Anne Frank need booking weeks out. Nights: Melkweg or Paradiso, both easy doors. De Pijp as your base.

Berlin · 3 nights

The most serious daytime city of the four — Wall Memorial, Topography of Terror, East Side Gallery, all free. Reichstag dome is free but needs registering weeks ahead with all six passport numbers. Nights: Sisyphos or Sunday-evening Berghain. Base yourselves in Friedrichshain.

Budapest · 3 nights

Cheapest city by a distance, so this is where you spend. Széchenyi baths by day, ruin bars by night, a Danube party boat with unlimited drinks for €58, and the Saturday Sparty. Stay in District VII, directly above the ruin bars.

Prague · 2 nights

Beer spa — oak tubs, unlimited self-poured beer, and Beerland's Suite 2 holds exactly six. €47 each. Prague Castle in the morning, Roxy or Cross Club at night, Náplavka riverbank in between. Stay in Holešovice or Vinohrady, not Old Town.

Weather — pack for this

Early October across all four cities: highs 13–17 °C, lows 6–9 °C, and it gets dark around 18:30. Amsterdam is the wettest and windiest. This is not shorts weather.

  • One warm layer plus a waterproof jacket — umbrellas are useless in Amsterdam wind
  • Boots or trainers you can stand in for six hours. Berlin clubs are concrete floors and long nights
  • Dark, plain clothing for Berlin doors. No football shirts, no suits, no going-out shirts
  • Swim shorts, flip-flops and a towel — mandatory for the Sparty and useful at the beer spa
  • One collared shirt each, for Prague's dressier clubs and any nice dinner

Bars, clubs and nights out

Re-researched 8 Aug 2026 against operator sites, city listings and Time Out. Two things drive everything below: which nights you're actually in each city — Berlin gets Sun/Mon/Tue and Prague gets Sun/Mon, both weak windows — and the fact that six men arriving together is the exact silhouette European door staff are trained to refuse.

Seven venues on the old plan are dead — don't route to them Watergate (Berlin) closed permanently end of 2024. Salon zur Wilden Renate survived and reopened April 2026, but runs Thu–Sat only, so it's shut all three of your Berlin nights. De School (Amsterdam) is permanently closed. Ellátó Kert (2019), Anker't (2020) and Corvintető (2018) are all long gone in Budapest — Corvin Palace is now Time Out Market. K5 Relax and OSSEGG are closed in Prague. Any 2026 "guide" still listing these is AI-generated filler; several do.
The rule that gets you in everywhere Never queue as six. Split into 2+2+2 and join the line 10–15 minutes apart. Don't wave or shout across the queue — if the door reads you as one unit of six, you're all out. If a bouncer tells you to split, split instantly; refusing is a documented instant rejection. Buy tickets online in advance, arrive sober, phones away in the line, and if you're refused say nothing and walk. Rejection rates of 60–80% are normal on a busy Berlin night and mean nothing about you.

Berlin · Sun 4 – Wed 7 Oct

Correction to the old plan: go to Berghain on Sunday AFTERNOON, not Sunday night Klubnacht for your weekend starts Sat 3 Oct at 23:59 and runs continuously to Mon 5 Oct around 08:00 — so your whole Sunday sits inside it. But the door is not uniform. Sunday 12:00–18:00 is the shortest queue and the most accessible door of the entire week, with entry rates reported at 65–80%. After 18:00 the door tightens again as the crowd shifts to regulars and reads tourist curiosity fast. Bouncer shifts change at 08:00 and 18:00, and a second attempt after a shift change is a known reset. Land, drop bags, be in line by 14:00–16:00.
Be realistic about six men at Berghain Every source converges: groups of four or more are refused near-systematically, and groups of six are put under 20%. One long-time local writes that he has never seen a group of four or more get in. Split into 2+2+2, queue apart, and plan for a split outcome — four of six getting in is a good night, not a failure. Agree an inside meeting point and agree what the ones who don't get in will do. €25–30 entry, €5 re-entry, drinks €4–7. Cloakroom €2.50 coat plus €1.50 bag access, cash, bring coins. Door staff sticker over both phone cameras; a group selfie in the queue is a documented instant rejection. Sunglasses come off at the door. Know one DJ on the bill.

Adults only

Four countries, four completely different legal regimes. In two of them the adult-entertainment scene is legal, licensed and priced on a board at the door. In the other two it is the single largest financial threat to your trip, and the documented failure mode is a four-figure bill enforced by security staff who will not let you leave. Read the Budapest and Prague sections before you go out, not after.

The one-paragraph version Amsterdam and Berlin: fine. Legal, regulated, priced upfront. Know the rules that carry fines and the dress codes that get you refused. Budapest and Prague: no. Not on moral grounds — on arithmetic. In both cities the venue category is unregulated or outright unlawful, which means there is no licence to complain to, no posted price, and no consumer-protection footing if a bill is disputed. Real documented bills: €630 for one beer in Budapest, ~$300 for a bottle of cheap champagne in Prague, with victims escorted to ATMs and held until they pay. Amsterdam — legal, licensed, and heavily policed

The law. Prostitution is legal and licensed — the brothel ban was lifted in October 2000. Operating a sex business requires a municipal licence, and sex workers are legally self-employed professionals who register with the Chamber of Commerce and pay tax. The national minimum age is 18, but Amsterdam's own bylaw requires 21. In May 2026 the cabinet announced plans to raise the national minimum to 21 and add a pimping ban — announced, not yet law.

The window system. Workers rent a window and room per shift from a licensed operator. Price and services are negotiated directly at the door before you enter — nothing is set by the house, and the commonly quoted street rate is around €50 upward for a short basic service. Hours, because most guides have this wrong. Window hours were cut to 03:00 in April 2023 and then restored to 06:00 by Mayor Halsema in February 2024 on worker-safety grounds. Berlin — legal and regulated, but the doors are the hardest of the trip

The law. Sex work is legal and regulated under the Prostituiertenschutzgesetz, in force since 1 July 2017. Registration is mandatory: workers hold a certificate valid two years (one if under 21), requiring in-person appearance, ID, proof of residency, a minimum age of 18 and a health consultation within the previous three months. Berlin is unusual in having no Sperrbezirk (restricted zone) and no curfew hours — though that last point rests on a single party-political source, so treat it as directional.

Budapest — the single biggest threat on this trip

The konzumlány / "consume girl" scam: organised, violent, and over €2.5 million taken This is Budapest's signature crime against groups of foreign men, and the mechanism is specifically designed so that a group is a liability rather than a protection. Hungarian police broke up one ring in 2025 that had taken over 1 billion HUF (~€2.5 million) from hundreds of victims, with arrests in February and June — a bar owner, a hostess and a bartender among them. Another ran for eighteen months with at least 19 foreign victims before being designated a criminal organisation. The rings get dismantled and re-form under new names, which is why no blacklist protects you and the pattern is the only defence.

How it actually works, stage by stage

1. The approach. One or two attractive, well-dressed, fluent-English-speaking women approach you — on Váci utca and around Vörösmarty tér, on Liszt Ferenc tér and Andrássy út, on the walking routes between the ruin bars, especially Kazinczy utca and the Danube quays at night. Increasingly it starts on dating apps and Instagram DMs before you even arrive. The opener is mundane: directions, a lighter, friendly conversation. After a few minutes she suggests a really cool bar she knows. Sometimes it's two women working two or three of you so it feels organic. Sometimes the friendly local is a man posing as a fellow tourist.

2. The venue. The bar, the bartender, the waiting staff, the security and the owner are all the same organisation — police describe a hierarchical structure controlled by bar owners. Complaining to the manager is complaining to the perpetrator. The signature is consistent: basement or lift-access only with no street frontage, no displayed menu or price list, nearly empty of other customers, an ATM inside the premises, and handwritten bills with no register receipt.

3. The consumption. She drinks freely and stays sober while you don't. Police noted victims were plied with alcohol while the women remained sober, causing them to lose judgment. Champagne appears. "Complimentary" shots appear. Drink spiking in Budapest bars is separately flagged by the UK FCDO.

4. The bill. Documented real cases below.

5. Enforcement — the part people underestimate. Large men appear the moment you dispute it; victims report being held in back rooms and physically intimidated. The FCDO states it plainly: "Tourists have been taken to cashpoints with demands for the money to pay the bill." Where there's more than one of you, one or two are walked to the ATM while the rest are held at the venue as collateral. Passport and card confiscation is documented. And your card is often run for a pre-authorisation hold before you ever see the bill, so the funds are ringfenced before you can object. Some taxi drivers take commission to deliver you — the FCDO says so explicitly.

Prague — a legal grey zone, which is exactly the problem

The law. Selling sex as an individual is not criminalised, but brothels, organising prostitution and pimping are all illegal. The Czech Interior Ministry counts 860-plus brothels nationally, around 200 in Prague — all technically unlawful, all operating openly, with enforcement not prioritised. Visa & logistics

The 45-day deadline is 17 August 2026 The Dutch government's instruction is to apply no later than 45 days before your trip, and its own published processing time for a short-stay Schengen visa is 25 calendar days on average — extending to 45 calendar days where an application needs further consideration or extra documents. Lodge around 20 August → passports back roughly 20–25 September, about a week of slack. Lodge in mid-September → you miss the trip. There is no expedited option for a holiday.

Why the Netherlands is the right consulate

Put this in the cover letter in one sentence — "the Netherlands is both the first point of entry and joint-longest stay" — so no officer has to work it out. At the border — EES, and why ETIAS doesn't apply to you

The EU Entry/Exit System has been fully operational since 10 April 2026. At Schiphol you'll follow the non-EU lane to either a self-service kiosk or a Marechaussee desk, where your passport data, face and fingerprints are captured. It replaces the passport stamp — expect no ink. The registration lasts three years.

Budget 60–90 minutes. Schiphol has 161 kiosks, but aviation bodies have reported waits of up to two hours at peaks, and six first-time registrations will not be quick. Don't plan anything tight for arrival afternoon.

ETIAS does not apply to you. It covers only visa-free nationalities. You hold a Schengen visa, so there is nothing to file and no €20 fee — and in any case ETIAS isn't mandatory until 2027. If an agent tries to sell you one, walk away.

NL→DE, DE→HU and HU→CZ are internal Schengen crossings with no formal checks — but the Netherlands and Germany have both reintroduced temporary internal border controls, so carry your passport at all times, including on the night train.

Worth asking for: a multi-entry visa

Under the 2024 EU–India cascade rules, anyone with two lawfully used Schengen visas in the past three years can be issued a two-year multi-entry visa, and later a five-year one. It costs nothing extra. Whoever qualifies should request it in the cover letter and attach copies of the previous visas plus entry/exit evidence. It won't change this trip — everything is inside Schengen — but it makes the next one trivial.

Prague — taxis, strip clubs, exchange booths Taxis: airport to centre is legitimately 600–800 CZK. Victims routinely pay 2,000–3,000. Use Bolt or Uber so the price is locked, and never take a cab parked outside a club, hotel or tourist square.

Strip clubs: touts on Na Příkopě, Wenceslas Square and Old Town Square offer "VIP access" or "free entry". Inside, hidden fees of 3,000 CZK+ per person appear and security escorts you to an ATM. Some run card-skimming alongside. This is the number one way groups like yours lose money in Prague. Assume every single one is a scam, because it is.

Exchange booths: headline rates near Old Town Square apply only to €500+, or carry hidden commission — effective markups of 15–20%. Some hand you Hungarian forint instead of koruna. Use bank ATMs only (ČSOB, Komerční, Česká spořitelna), never Euronet, and always decline dynamic currency conversion.
Restaurant bills in Prague and Budapest Items you didn't order appear on the bill — bread, olives, and at U Fleků a shot of Becherovka placed on your table unasked at ~90 CZK. Refuse it the moment it arrives or you're paying for it. Meat priced per 100g turns a "180 CZK" pork knuckle into 700 CZK. Many Budapest restaurants auto-add a 10–15% szervízdíj — check before tipping on top. Read the bill line by line, every time.
Amsterdam — fines and fake drugs The city runs an official "Stay Away" campaign explicitly targeting young male party tourists. Enforcement toward your exact demographic is deliberate, not incidental.
  • Drinking alcohol in the street: €95–140
  • Smoking cannabis outdoors in De Wallen, Dam, Damrak, Nieuwmarkt: €100
  • Photographing sex workers: €100 — the most-enforced rule in the city, and phones get grabbed
  • Public urination: €150 · Noise in residential streets: €150–450 · Nitrous possession: €250

Never buy from a street dealer. Counterfeit product around De Wallen — the well-documented case being white heroin sold as cocaine — has killed tourists. Nothing sold on the street is what it claims to be. Coffeeshops are 18+, need a passport, max 5g per purchase.
Berlin — the rules that catch tourists out
  • Quiet hours, 22:00–06:00 daily plus all day Sunday, are legally enforceable in an apartment. Six men coming home loudly at 05:00 gets the police called and can get your booking cancelled. Silence in the stairwell.
  • Sat 3 and Sun 4 October are both closed days — Unity Day then Sunday. Supermarkets shut. Restaurants, bars, clubs and Spätis stay open.
  • Jaywalking is an actual offence (€5–10) and locals will tell you off. Crossing on red in front of children is treated more seriously.
  • Nazi salutes and symbols are a criminal offence, including as a joke for a photo. Tourists are prosecuted for this every year. Don't.
  • Görlitzer Park after dark: no. Kottbusser Tor and Warschauer Straße late at night: keep your wits.
