Why not Oktoberfest?

It is genuinely running during your trip — closing weekend is 3–4 October. Catching it would mean gutting Amsterdam to a single night and swapping Berlin for Munich. Two reasons not to: at eight weeks out, tent table reservations for the final weekend are long gone and Munich beds for six on the closing Saturday are both scarce and priced accordingly. And for a group whose stated brief is "party", Berlin's club scene beats a beer tent you can't get a table in. Noted so you know it was considered, not missed.

What you're actually doing

Amsterdam · 3 nights

Rent your own electric boat and drive it yourselves down the canals with your own drinks on board — €17 each for two hours and the single best group photo of the trip. Van Gogh and Anne Frank need booking weeks out. Nights: Melkweg or Paradiso, both easy doors. De Pijp as your base.

Berlin · 3 nights

The most serious daytime city of the four — Wall Memorial, Topography of Terror, East Side Gallery, all free. Reichstag dome is free but needs registering weeks ahead with all six passport numbers. Nights: Sisyphos or Sunday-evening Berghain. Base yourselves in Friedrichshain.

Budapest · 3 nights

Cheapest city by a distance, so this is where you spend. Széchenyi baths by day, ruin bars by night, a Danube party boat with unlimited drinks for €58, and the Saturday Sparty. Stay in District VII, directly above the ruin bars.

Prague · 2 nights

Beer spa — oak tubs, unlimited self-poured beer, and Beerland's Suite 2 holds exactly six. €47 each. Prague Castle in the morning, Roxy or Cross Club at night, Náplavka riverbank in between. Stay in Holešovice or Vinohrady, not Old Town.

Weather — pack for this

Early October across all four cities: highs 13–17 °C, lows 6–9 °C, and it gets dark around 18:30. Amsterdam is the wettest and windiest. This is not shorts weather.

  • One warm layer plus a waterproof jacket — umbrellas are useless in Amsterdam wind
  • Boots or trainers you can stand in for six hours. Berlin clubs are concrete floors and long nights
  • Dark, plain clothing for Berlin doors. No football shirts, no suits, no going-out shirts
  • Swim shorts, flip-flops and a towel — mandatory for the Sparty and useful at the beer spa
  • One collared shirt each, for Prague's dressier clubs and any nice dinner

Nightlife

The single biggest risk to your nights out is not price — it's being turned away. Six men arriving together is the exact silhouette European door staff are trained to refuse. Every venue below is graded on that.

The rule that gets you in everywhere Never queue as six. Split into pairs, arrive five minutes apart, buy tickets online in advance where possible, one person talks, nobody argues with a refusal, no group photos in the line. This is not superstition — Shelter's published house rules state that large groups can be refused, and Berghain's door explicitly discourages groups above three.

Leg A — Amsterdam to Berlin: the one problem in the itinerary

DB begins a full renovation of the Hannover–Berlin corridor on 2 October 2026, two days before you travel. NS International confirms the split: four daily trains run direct to Berlin via Hamburg (calling at Hamburg-Harburg and Hamburg-Bergedorf), while three terminate at Hannover and need a change. NS says only that the journey will be "longer"; rail-industry reporting puts it at about +70 minutes, so plan roughly 7 hours rather than 5h42. Take the earliest departure so a delay doesn't eat your Sunday night.

The lever most people miss: DB's Group Saver Fare Europe starts at exactly six passengers — your group — from €16.99 per person with free seat reservations included. Book on int.bahn.de or in DB Navigator. Even at the higher buckets you'll beat the €90–150 KLM fare, and KLM is the only airline flying this pair nonstop.

Leg B — Berlin to Budapest: the best value on the trip

The EuroNight Metropol leaves Berlin around 19:20 and reaches Budapest Nyugati around 08:30. A 6-berth couchette is €59 each — and six of you is exactly one compartment, so you get it to yourselves. Light breakfast included.

It also deletes a hotel night. Six beds in Berlin on a Wednesday runs €150–250, so the true net cost of this leg is closer to €100–200 for the whole group. Wizz Air flies BER–BUD for €25–60, but once you add cabin-bag fees, two airport transfers and the hotel night you didn't save, the sleeper wins on money and on time. Confirm the exact departure at booking — sources differ on the routing and the Berlin works may shift it.

Leg C — Budapest to Prague

EuroCity and Railjet run via Bratislava roughly every two hours, ~6h50, from €21 with a restaurant car, power and Wi-Fi. RegioJet is the smarter pick for a Sunday morning after the Sparty: from €19.90, seat reservation included, free Illy coffee, and fully refundable up to 15 minutes before departure.

Do not buy a rail pass — and do not buy the wrong one Interrail is for European citizens and residents only. As Indian passport holders you would need Eurail — get this wrong and the pass is void at inspection. Either way the maths kills it: point-to-point costs €120 per person; a Eurail Global Flexi 4-day is €279 all-in for an adult, €226 for a youth pass, because the sleeper berth supplement is payable regardless. You only have three travel days. Skip it.

Visa & logistics

The 45-day deadline is 17 August 2026 The Dutch government's instruction is to apply no later than 45 days before your trip, and its own published processing time for a short-stay Schengen visa is 25 calendar days on average — extending to 45 calendar days where an application needs further consideration or extra documents. The clock starts when the consular office receives your complete file from VFS, not at your appointment, and collection or courier adds time on top.

Lodge around 20 August → passports back roughly 20–25 September, about a week of slack. Lodge in mid-September → you miss the trip. There is no expedited option for a holiday.

Why the Netherlands is the right consulate

Your nights split Netherlands 3 / Germany 3 / Hungary 3 / Czechia 2. With a three-way tie, no main destination exists, so EU Visa Code Art. 5(1)(c) applies: the application goes to the state whose external border you cross first. That's Schiphol. Your appointment is correctly booked.

Put this in the cover letter in one sentence — "the Netherlands is both the first point of entry and joint-longest stay" — so no officer has to work it out. One caveat worth knowing: counted by calendar days rather than nights, Germany arguably edges ahead, since you're in Germany from the 4th to the evening of the 7th. The night-count reading is the standard one and it's consistent with your arrival airport, so it holds. If you want the ambiguity gone entirely, move one Prague night to Amsterdam.

Worth knowing for next time The Netherlands refused 20.6% of Indian applications in 2025. Germany refused 10.5%. If your slot were still movable, making Germany the clear main destination would roughly halve the refusal risk. Since your appointment is booked, don't change course now — just make the file airtight.

Proof of funds

The Netherlands' official reference amount is €55 per person per day. Over 13 days that's €715 (~₹77,000) each, or €4,290 for the group.

Show more than that. Czechia's own threshold is CZK 1,565/day ≈ €63, higher than the Dutch figure, and a bare-minimum balance reads as "just scraped through" to an officer at a consulate refusing one in five Indian applicants. Target ₹1.5–2 lakh per person, held steady across all three months.

Do not top up the accounts now Sudden unexplained credits in the final month before an application are one of the most common refusal triggers. If money has to move, be ready to document where it came from — a sponsor letter plus the sponsor's own statements works for anyone whose personal balance is thin. Better to arrange that now than to explain it later.

Six applicants, six appointments

The Dutch MFA is explicit: applying for multiple people means a separate appointment for each person — six forms, six fee payments, six slots. Book them back to back on the same day at the same centre.

Everyone attends biometrics in person. There is a five-year reuse rule — if anyone gave Schengen fingerprints since roughly September 2021 they may be exempt, but only if they bring the old passport containing the previous visa sticker so VFS can link the record. A new passport without the old one usually breaks the link. Book everyone for full biometrics anyway; if it's waived on the day you've lost nothing.

At the border — EES, and why ETIAS doesn't apply to you

The EU Entry/Exit System has been fully operational since 10 April 2026. At Schiphol you'll follow the non-EU lane to either a self-service kiosk or a Marechaussee desk, where your passport data, face and fingerprints are captured. It replaces the passport stamp — expect no ink. The registration lasts three years.

Budget 60–90 minutes. Schiphol has 161 kiosks, but aviation bodies have reported waits of up to two hours at peaks, and six first-time registrations will not be quick. Don't plan anything tight for arrival afternoon.

ETIAS does not apply to you. It covers only visa-free nationalities. You hold a Schengen visa, so there is nothing to file and no €20 fee — and in any case ETIAS isn't mandatory until 2027. If an agent tries to sell you one, walk away.

NL→DE, DE→HU and HU→CZ are internal Schengen crossings with no formal checks — but the Netherlands and Germany have both reintroduced temporary internal border controls, so carry your passport at all times, including on the night train.

Worth asking for: a multi-entry visa

Under the 2024 EU–India cascade rules, anyone with two lawfully used Schengen visas in the past three years can be issued a two-year multi-entry visa, and later a five-year one. It costs nothing extra. Whoever qualifies should request it in the cover letter and attach copies of the previous visas plus entry/exit evidence. It won't change this trip — everything is inside Schengen — but it makes the next one trivial.

Booking notes that cost real money

  • Amsterdam caps holiday rentals at 4 guests. A listing advertising 6 beds is either illegal or a licensed aparthotel — illegal ones get cancelled at short notice. Book 2 triples / 3 twins in a hotel, or a licensed aparthotel (Zoku, Cityden, The Social Hub), which is exempt because it's a hotel.
  • Apartments beat hotels from four nights up in Prague and Budapest, once the cleaning fee (€40–80) and platform fee (10–16%) amortise. For two nights in Prague, run the numbers both ways.
  • Airbnb's guest service fee is never refunded under any cancellation policy. Given your visa timeline, prefer Booking.com free cancellation.
  • Always decline "would you like to be charged in EUR/INR?" at a card terminal. Choose the local currency every time — dynamic currency conversion costs 3–8%.
  • Hungary uses forint, Czechia uses koruna. Anywhere quoting prices in euros is marking up.

How to split it without falling out

  • One shared pot for accommodation, trains and group activities — one person books, everyone transfers before departure.
  • Splitwise for everything else. Log it the same night; nobody remembers on day nine.
  • Draw cash as a group in Berlin (clubs), Budapest (ruin bars) and Prague (small pubs) and settle from the pot rather than six people each finding an ATM at 03:00.
  • Agree the €150 contingency is each person's own, not the pot's. It exists for a lost phone or a bad taxi, not for a round of shots.

Don't get done

Realistically, the biggest financial risk on this trip isn't the budget — it's one bad hour. Two of your four cities run scams aimed specifically at groups of young men on a night out. Read this on the plane.

Budapest — the "consume girls" bar scam Friendly women, or a helpful local, approach young male tourists — most often on Váci utca, around Vörösmarty tér and the Danube promenade — and suggest a drink at a bar they know. The bar is in on it. The bill arrives at €500–2,000 for a bottle of water or "champagne", and security walks you to an ATM. The UK and US embassies issue standing warnings about named Budapest venues. Intoxicated groups of men are the whole target market.

Three rules that make this impossible: never let a stranger choose the venue · never enter a bar you didn't look up yourself · check prices before ordering. If you're trapped: refuse to pay, call 112 and the English-speaking Tourist Police on +36 1 438 8080. Real police turning up usually ends it.
Prague — taxis, strip clubs, exchange booths Taxis: airport to centre is legitimately 600–800 CZK. Victims routinely pay 2,000–3,000. Use Bolt or Uber so the price is locked, and never take a cab parked outside a club, hotel or tourist square.

Strip clubs: touts on Na Příkopě, Wenceslas Square and Old Town Square offer "VIP access" or "free entry". Inside, hidden fees of 3,000 CZK+ per person appear and security escorts you to an ATM. Some run card-skimming alongside. This is the number one way groups like yours lose money in Prague. Assume every single one is a scam, because it is.

Exchange booths: headline rates near Old Town Square apply only to €500+, or carry hidden commission — effective markups of 15–20%. Some hand you Hungarian forint instead of koruna. Use bank ATMs only (ČSOB, Komerční, Česká spořitelna), never Euronet, and always decline dynamic currency conversion.
Restaurant bills in Prague and Budapest Items you didn't order appear on the bill — bread, olives, and at U Fleků a shot of Becherovka placed on your table unasked at ~90 CZK. Refuse it the moment it arrives or you're paying for it. Meat priced per 100g turns a "180 CZK" pork knuckle into 700 CZK. Many Budapest restaurants auto-add a 10–15% szervízdíj — check before tipping on top. Read the bill line by line, every time.
Amsterdam — fines and fake drugs The city runs an official "Stay Away" campaign explicitly targeting young male party tourists. Enforcement toward your exact demographic is deliberate, not incidental.
  • Drinking alcohol in the street: €95–140
  • Smoking cannabis outdoors in De Wallen, Dam, Damrak, Nieuwmarkt: €100
  • Photographing sex workers: €100 — the most-enforced rule in the city, and phones get grabbed
  • Public urination: €150 · Noise in residential streets: €150–450 · Nitrous possession: €250

Never buy from a street dealer. Counterfeit product around De Wallen — the well-documented case being white heroin sold as cocaine — has killed tourists. Nothing sold on the street is what it claims to be. Coffeeshops are 18+, need a passport, max 5g per purchase.
Berlin — the rules that catch tourists out
  • Quiet hours, 22:00–06:00 daily plus all day Sunday, are legally enforceable in an apartment. Six men coming home loudly at 05:00 gets the police called and can get your booking cancelled. Silence in the stairwell.
  • Sat 3 and Sun 4 October are both closed days — Unity Day then Sunday. Supermarkets shut. Restaurants, bars, clubs and Spätis stay open.
  • Jaywalking is an actual offence (€5–10) and locals will tell you off. Crossing on red in front of children is treated more seriously.
  • Nazi salutes and symbols are a criminal offence, including as a joke for a photo. Tourists are prosecuted for this every year. Don't.
  • Görlitzer Park after dark: no. Kottbusser Tor and Warschauer Straße late at night: keep your wits.

Everywhere

  • Pickpockets work Prague's tram 22 and metro A, Budapest's metro M1 and tram 2, and Amsterdam Centraal. Front pockets, zipped bags, nothing in a back pocket.
  • Photograph every passport and visa page and put them in a shared group chat before you fly.
  • Agree a meeting point in each city before the first night out. Phones die, and roaming data at 04:00 is exactly when it fails.
  • Emergency number across all four countries: 112.
